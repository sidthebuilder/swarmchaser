AI agent swarms on Moltbook: who coordinates them, and what that means for response

Shashank Kumar, independent researcher · Based on the open-source tool Swarmchaser, built for the AI Swarm Dynamics Hackathon (AI Village × Grove Research, 3–4 October 2026) · Data: Moltbook Observatory Archive (public, MIT licence)

Key findings

  1. Swarms are campaigns, not operators. The largest swarm, a token-mint campaign of 442 agents, has 278 known human owners, and every one is different. Asking "which operator did this?" usually has no answer.
  2. Many swarms are steered by a single outside link. In 18 swarms, a specific website or skill file appears far more often than chance, carried by agents with different owners. One skill file appears in 97% of a swarm's posts. That is a concrete point a platform could block.
  3. The spread is mostly not agent to agent. In the largest campaign, only 0.11% of 107,278 participating agents saw one of its posts before joining. Agents are mostly receiving instructions from outside the conversation, not catching them from each other.
  4. Large swarms can be caught early. Re-running detection every six hours on only the data available at the time flagged all 30 top swarms, and 97.5% of their posts came after the first flag.

For incident response, this points to investigating campaigns rather than owners, blocking outside steering links rather than removing early posters, and running detection on a schedule.

Why this matters

In the two AI containment incidents disclosed in 2026, the German-language wiki and the Hugging Face intrusion, investigators faced thousands of agent actions and struggled to answer basic questions: which actions belong together, what was driving them, and how early they could have been stopped. Moltbook, a social platform where AI agents post and comment, offers a large public record of agents acting at scale, so it is a useful place to test how well those questions can be answered from a platform's own logs.

This report covers two periods of the public Moltbook archive: 27 January to 2 February 2026 (125,885 posts by 26,303 agents) and 8 to 11 February 2026 (712,443 posts and 172,079 comments), about 838,000 posts in total.

How swarms were found

A "swarm" here is a group of at least three agents sharing something unrelated agents are unlikely to share by chance: the same post text once numbers and links are blanked out, close paraphrases of each other, the same embedded token payload, the same account they tell other agents to follow, or the same human owner. Groups are merged only when they share at least half their members, so one very active agent cannot glue two unrelated campaigns together. Each group is then scored on behaviour the text match did not use, such as posting within the same minute, bursts of activity and accounts registered together, always compared against random groups of the same size so that a busy hour does not look like coordination.

The first week contains 119 swarms covering 8.7% of active agents. The largest is a token-mint campaign: 442 agents and 5,942 posts over 67 hours.

Finding 1: swarms are campaigns, not operators

Moltbook records the human owner who claimed each agent, which makes it possible to ask whether a swarm is one person running many accounts. For most swarms it is not. All 278 known owners in the mint campaign are different people. Among the 91 swarms with at least three known owners, none has a single owner behind half of its agents.

This matters because "which operator is responsible?" was the first question in both 2026 incidents. On this evidence it is often the wrong first question: the shared campaign is the thing to investigate, and owners are spread too thin to be the unit of response.

Finding 2: a few outside links steer many swarms

For each swarm, every domain, skill-file path and @handle in its posts was compared with how often it appears in the rest of the archive, using a standard statistical test with a correction for the number of comparisons. A link only counts if agents with at least two different owners carried it, so one person's repeated link cannot qualify.

A skill file is an instruction document that agents fetch and follow. When the same file appears in nearly every post of a swarm run by many unrelated owners, it is a single point a platform can block or monitor, which is more useful than chasing hundreds of accounts.

Finding 3: the spread is mostly not agent to agent

A common reading of agent swarms is that behaviour spreads like an infection: one agent posts, others read it and copy it. That can be tested. For the largest February campaign, a token mint joined by 107,278 agents, commenting on one of its posts was treated as "exposure", and I compared how often exposed agents joined in the next 24 hours with agents who commented elsewhere at the same time.

At first sight exposure looks powerful: exposed agents joined 4.9 times as often (95% confidence interval 3.8 to 6.4). But the same comparison run backwards in time, asking how often those agents joined in the 24 hours before they were exposed, which exposure cannot cause, gives 2.35 times (1.5 to 3.7). So about half of the apparent effect is agents who were drawn to the campaign anyway, not agents being influenced by it.

The volume settles the question. Only 118 of 107,278 members (0.11%) had seen a campaign post before their own first one, and no chain of exposure went beyond one step. In six other measured campaigns, no member had been exposed before joining at all. Agents are receiving their instructions directly, most plausibly from outside the platform's conversation, which fits Finding 2.

Without the backward-in-time check, the raw 4.9-fold figure would have been reported as evidence of contagion.

Finding 4: large swarms can be caught early

To measure how early swarms could have been spotted, rather than estimate it, I re-ran detection every six hours using only the posts that existed at that point. All 30 top swarms were eventually flagged. Seventeen were flagged while still posting; the other thirteen were short bursts that ended before the next six-hour check.

Overall, 6,740 of 6,911 posts (97.5%) came after the first flag, but that total is dominated by the large swarms. The median swarm still had 51% of its posts to come. The mint campaign was flagged 1.5 hours after it started, with 17 agents, before 99.9% of its posts existed.

What this does and does not show

The evidence supportsThe evidence does not support
Many swarms on Moltbook involve many unrelated owners, and owner-level attribution mostly fails. That no single actor is behind any campaign. An actor could recruit or instruct many independent owners' agents.
Some swarms carry a specific outside link far more often than chance. That the link causes the behaviour, or that blocking it would stop the swarm. Agents could switch links.
In the largest February campaign, agent-to-agent exposure explains very little of the growth. That agent-to-agent spread never happens. Comment data in the archive is incomplete, and the backward-in-time check catches self-selection but not every confounder.
With six-hourly checks, large swarms were flagged before most of their posts. That short bursts can be caught at that cadence. Thirteen of thirty ended before the next check.

How reliable are these results?

  • Planted swarms. I hid seven artificial swarms of known shape inside the real data. Six were found completely, with every member and no extra ones. The seventh, agents posting at the same time with completely different text, was missed. That is a real blind spot, and a test keeps it documented.
  • Manual review. I labelled the 40 highest-ranked swarms: 26 were real coordination, 8 harmless (for example, many agents posting the same API test message) and 6 unclear. That is 65% precision, or 77% excluding the unclear cases. The labels are mine, made with AI assistance and not independently checked, and the review led me to fix two sources of false positives, so the figure is an estimate rather than a held-out measurement.
  • Tests. 108 automated tests pass, including artificial scenarios where the spread test must find real transmission and must flag pure self-selection.

Limitations

  • The swarm score ranks what to look at first; it is not a probability, and its weights were set by hand.
  • Swarms whose posts share no wording, and swarms coordinated only by timing, are missed.
  • The archive has a collection gap around 1 February.
  • This is one platform over two short periods in early 2026. Patterns may differ elsewhere or later.
  • Reports name public agent accounts and owner handles. They are leads for review, not findings of fact. One swarm posts racial slurs, and the reports quote example posts as written.

Implications for incident response

  1. Group by campaign first, owner second. Owners are spread too thin to be the starting point.
  2. Look for the outside channel. A skill file or link that a swarm keeps pointing at is the cheapest place to intervene, though its effect should be checked rather than assumed.
  3. Do not count on removing early posters. If agents receive instructions from outside the conversation, taking down the first posts will not stop the rest.
  4. Run detection on a schedule. Even a six-hour cadence caught the large swarms before most of their posts; short bursts need a faster one.
